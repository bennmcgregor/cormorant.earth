The experience was like walking through a minefield. There are so many ways giving an opinion online can go wrong! This was my friend's idea to "feed the beast", and I thought the exercise would be worth it. And it was. It started a whole conversation [in the YouTube comments](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/B2zFri0KRUg). Apolline said:

> In French, we call the highly skilled, talented fashion workers in fashion houses "petites mains" (little hands). Many French haute couture designers and luxury brands like Chanel or Hermés are keeping many dying craft alive but indirectly keep the work of these women and men mostly hidden. When you talked about 200 hours I could not help but wonder when those 200 hours began. 200 hours combined or 200 hours of an entire workshop working at once ? Did the clock start running when the fabric was harvested, woven, embroidered or put together by the designer ?

And that got me thinking: what does it actually take to make a Met Gala dress? My response:

> that is so very interesting… and sounds like a really cool video idea, learning about the dying crafts that these fashion houses keep alive. I will definitely look into this further. I wonder if there are craftspeople here in New York employed in that capacity…

This is the kind of reporting I could find on the process: https://www.vogue.com/slideshow/hidden-details-behind-your-favorite-met-gala-looks. That's to say, very little, and most of it surface level (although the surface level stats are impressive!).

Are there craftspeople employed by big fashion houses in NYC? What are the dying crafts that are being kept alive? I read somewhere/heard somewhere that craft these days is more internet based. There mostly isn’t a "traditional" craft that we inherit. It makes me wonder if that’s a fallacy… what is that a symptom of?

How does this connect to/parallel the rise of AI?
